Lyria review: Google's free full-song model, with a length cap

Reviewed 9 November 2026 Updated 9 November 2026 PickWorth Editorial

The short version

In our runs the section tags were honoured, a prompt naming a BPM and a key came back arranged that way, and full-song generations returned in about 35 to 50 seconds. The deduction is not the sound, it is the fence around it. For a jingle, a bed, a podcast intro or a compliance-sensitive ad, Lyria 3.5 is the default; for a long singalong or a heavier genre, switch to Suno. What is Lyria 3.5?
Lyria 3.5 is Google DeepMind's text-to-music and image-to-music model, the successor to Lyria 3 Pro. It takes one prompt with genre, instruments, tempo, key, lyrics and section tags, plus up to ten images, and returns a complete song with vocals or an instrumental at 44.1 kHz stereo, in MP3 or WAV. The same lineage powered MusicFX and the AI Test Kitchen, and it now sits inside Google Flow Music, the Gemini app, Google AI Studio and the Gemini API.
How much of Lyria is free?
A lot. In the Gemini app anyone 18 or older gets the model within usage limits, and Google Flow Music has a free plan with daily top-up credits and WAV plus stem downloads. We counted about 500 Flow Music credits on a fresh sign-up, enough for roughly 15 to 50 songs depending on length. The Gemini API is the paid route with no free tier: 0.08 dollars a song for a full track, 0.04 dollars for a 30-second clip.
What is Lyria 3.5 good at?
Structure and control. We found section tags like Verse and Chorus are honoured, and a prompt that names a BPM, a key and a vocal style comes back arranged that way rather than as a generic loop. It is the only frontier music model a developer can buy, it sings in the language of the prompt across eight languages, and it composes against up to ten images. The licensed-data training is the quiet advantage: Suno and Udio face label litigation, so a client's legal team sleeps better on a Lyria track.
Where does Lyria 3.5 slip?
The fence around the song is real. Every track stops at three minutes, so nothing longer comes out in one pass. On the API each request is single-turn: no follow-up edits, no batch discount, and a failed take is still billed. We asked for exactly 60 seconds and the audible music stopped at about 59.6 seconds, so a locked export needs trimming. Prompts that name a specific artist's voice are blocked, and harder styles tend to come back acoustic-leaning.
How much does Lyria 3.5 cost?
In the Gemini app it is free within usage limits. Google Flow Music Free gives daily top-up credits; Starter is $8 a month for 3,000 credits, about 600 songs; Plus is $24 a month for 10,000 credits, about 2,000 songs; Member is $64 a month for 30,000 credits, about 6,000 songs. Yearly billing takes 25% off, to roughly $6, $18 and $48 a month. On the Gemini API a full song is $0.08 and a 30-second clip is $0.04, with no free tier. Re-check Google's live pricing, because regional listings move.
Lyria 3.5 vs Suno vs Udio: which should I use?
Pick by the risk and the length. Lyria 3.5 is the licensed-data, free-in-Flow-Music option with the tightest structural control, capped at three minutes. Suno wins on song length, up to about eight minutes on the top tier, and on a catchier mainstream hook with less effort, but it carries label litigation. Udio leans on audio fidelity and instrument separation with weaker prompt control. If you are publishing commercially and want provenance, Lyria 3.5; if you want a long singalong, Suno.

What it is

Lyria 3.5 is Google DeepMind's music generation model, the successor to Lyria 3 Pro, reached through Google Flow Music, the Gemini app, Google AI Studio and the Gemini API. Its defining move is that it writes a whole song in one pass: genre, instruments, tempo, key, mood, and a verse-chorus-bridge structure, with vocals and lyrics the model either writes or reads from your prompt. That is a step up from the short instrumental clips of the MusicFX and AI Test Kitchen era, which is why a tool that once made 30-second textures now hands you a three-minute song.

The model takes one text prompt and up to ten images, and returns a track at 44.1 kHz stereo in MP3 or WAV, with lyrics and structure logged alongside the audio. The prompt does the steering: section tags like Verse and Chorus are honoured, timestamps can pin a drop or an intro, and a named genre, BPM and vocal style comes back arranged that way. Every output carries an inaudible SynthID watermark, and Google says the model is trained on licensed data, which is the quiet advantage over its main rivals.

What it is good at

Structural control

This is the feature that separates Lyria 3.5 from the field we ran. It reads a prompt the way a session player reads a chart. We wrote a prompt naming a tempo of 72 BPM, a key of D minor, a female alto with a warm lower range and a section list of Intro, Verse, Chorus, Bridge, Outro, and the returned song followed that plan rather than wandering into a generic loop. For a client brief that needs a specific rise and a clean landing, that control is worth more than a small gain in raw audio quality.

Licensed-data provenance

Google says Lyria trains on licensed music data, and it is the only frontier model that does so without an active label suit. Suno and Udio are in litigation with major labels over their training data; a Lyria track arrives with that risk already handled at the model, plus a SynthID mark that survives compression, pitch shift and remix. For a commercial ad or a client deliverable, that is the difference between a track you can clear and one you have to explain.

Free access through Flow Music

In the Gemini app anyone 18 or older gets the model within usage limits, and Google Flow Music has a free plan with daily top-up credits and WAV plus stem downloads. We counted about 500 Flow Music credits on a fresh sign-up, enough for roughly 15 to 50 songs depending on length. That is a genuinely usable free route for a jingle or a bed, not a token trial.

Where it slips

The constraints are structural. Every track stops at three minutes, so nothing longer comes out in one pass, and a five-minute song needs an extend step that adds time and can drift. On the API each request is single-turn: no follow-up edits, no batch discount, no seed to reproduce a take, and a failed generation is still billed. The safety filter blocks prompts that ask for a specific artist's voice or copyrighted lyrics, and in our runs two of five such prompts returned nothing. The sound leans acoustic and mainstream: a hard-rock request came back as a rock-flavoured take with softer guitars, so EDM, metal and aggressive styles read tame. We did not test non-English singing beyond the eight documented languages, and we have not verified distribution-level detection, which needs a platform account.

Lyria vs Suno vs Udio

Pick by the risk and the length. Lyria 3.5 is the licensed-data, free-in-Flow-Music option with the tightest structural control, capped at three minutes and watermarked with SynthID. Suno wins on length, up to about eight minutes on its top tier, and on a catchier mainstream hook with less effort, but it carries label litigation and its prompt adherence is looser. Udio leans on audio fidelity and instrument separation with weaker control over structure. If you are publishing commercially and want provenance, Lyria 3.5; if you want a long singalong or a heavier genre, Suno; if you are chasing raw fidelity and are happy to hand-edit, Udio.

Verdict

Lyria 3.5 is the AI music model to reach for when the deliverable has to come with a clean provenance story, and the reason is narrow but decisive: it is the only frontier music model a developer can buy, it is trained on licensed data, and it writes a structured song with vocals from a single prompt. In our runs the section tags were honoured and full-song generations returned in about 35 to 50 seconds. The deduction is not the sound, it is the fence: three minutes, single-turn API at 0.08 dollars a song with no free tier, a small duration drift on timed exports, and a SynthID watermark you cannot remove. For a jingle, a bed or a compliance-sensitive ad it is the default; for a long song or a harder genre, switch to Suno.
