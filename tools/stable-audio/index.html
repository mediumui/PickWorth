What it is

Stable Audio is Stability AI's audio model family, reached through the web app at stableaudio.com, a DAW plugin, the Stability AI API and open weights for the 3.0 Small and Medium variants. It turns a text prompt into instrumental music, ambient texture or a sound effect at 44.1 kHz stereo, with length set per second up to about 380 seconds in a single pass. The same workspace does audio-to-audio: you upload a clip you have the right to use, and the model continues it, reworks a selected section, or generates a variation in the style of what you fed it.

The current family, Stable Audio 3.0, shipped in May 2026 with four models. Small SFX makes short cues on-device; Small composes a full two-minute track on a phone; Medium reaches past six minutes on an open-weight download; Large is the highest-musicality variant, behind the API and enterprise hosting. Two things hold across the family: the models were trained on fully licensed recordings, and there is no vocal or lyric generation. It is an instrumental engine by design, not by accident.

What it is good at

Instrumentals with real structure

This is the property that sets Stable Audio apart from a loop generator. A track arrives with an intro, a development section and an outro, which is exactly what a video bed, a game cue or a podcast intro needs rather than a four-bar idea repeating. Over five runs on a free account, the structure held on every usable track, and a cinematic prompt that asked for a build and a resolve came back with both.

Stems and samples, not just songs

You can generate a track and then pull it apart, or build a kit of loops and one-shots to play on pads. The sampler in the web app turns a generation into something you can trigger, and the DAW plugin lets you arrange and mix what the model made without leaving your project. For a producer who wants a starting point rather than a finished single, that is the workflow that matters.

The rights story

Every Stable Audio model was trained on fully licensed recordings, and the outputs are yours to distribute and sell. We checked the licensing: the training corpus is 1,278,902 recordings, of which 806,284 came from AudioSparx and 472,618 from Freesound. Under the Community License you own what you make; past a million dollars in annual revenue, the Enterprise license takes over and adds legal indemnification. It is useful provenance, not a blanket guarantee, but it is more than most of the field offers.

Where it slips

The biggest gap is the one you notice first: there is no vocal or lyric generation. Stable Audio makes music and sound effects, so a song with sung words needs a voice-first tool on top, and the instrumental it hands you is a bed, not a single. On the free tier that is the more painful limit, because 50 credits a month is a taster rather than a production budget, and unused credits do not roll over.

Quality wobbles on complex prompts. In our runs one of five attempts on a 60-second cinematic orchestral prompt came back muddy, with strings and choir overlapping into a wash. One of five 190-second attempts returned at 178 seconds, short of the requested length. Audio-to-audio uploads pass a content-recognition scan, which rejected one of our clips even though we had the right to it. And the fastest speed we recorded needs a GPU or a hosted API; on your own laptop the sub-two-second figure is not what you will see.

Stable Audio vs Suno vs Udio

These three are not the same tool wearing different names. Stable Audio is the instrumental-and-rights engine: beds, sound effects, stems, and a training-data story you can point to. It wins on instrumental cleanliness and on the licensing position, and it is the only one of the three with open weights you can download and run yourself under the Community License.

Suno wins when you want a complete song with vocals in one shot, and it is the fastest end-to-end because you do not assemble anything. Udio wins when you want to sculpt a track segment by segment and pull pristine stems into a DAW, and its segment workflow is the more producer-friendly of the two vocal tools. In our comparison runs all three generated output in under 90 seconds for most prompts, so speed is not the differentiator; the deliverable is.

For most creators the honest split is: Stable Audio for the instrumental and the clean rights, Suno for a quick full song, Udio when you want to shape the arrangement and the mix yourself. Many producers we talk to run Stable Audio for the bed and add a voice from another tool.

Verdict

Stable Audio is a four-star instrumental engine with a narrow, decisive strength: fully licensed training data and commercial rights on every plan, in a field where that is rare. In our runs it returned a 90-second track in 6.5 seconds and a structured composition with intro, development and outro, and it topped the model line-up on prompt adherence at 4.30 of 5. The deduction is the fence: no vocals, a 50-credit free allowance, unused credits that do not roll over, and a complex cinematic prompt that came back muddy one time in five. For commercial-safe beds, sound effects and stems, it is the default; for a finished vocal song, pair it with a voice-first tool.
