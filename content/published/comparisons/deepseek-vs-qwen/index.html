Head-to-head

DeepSeek vs Qwen

Updated 26 October 2026 PickWorth Editorial
4.2DeepSeek score

Who wins

Neither is the other's replacement. DeepSeek is the better default for anyone who mostly wants to call a strong reasoning-and-coding model through an API without watching the meter: its V4 pricing is a fraction of Qwen's flagship, the licence is plain MIT, and the coding scores are a hair ahead. Qwen is the better pick when the job is broader than one endpoint — when you need a 201-language model, a small Apache-2.0 checkpoint for on-premise work, or a single vendor that also covers vision, audio and video. Score the pairing 4.2 and call DeepSeek the higher-rated tool, but only because most comparison readers are shopping for an API, not a model zoo.

The short answer. DeepSeek wins on price and raw reasoning-and-code value: its V4 API is several times cheaper than Qwen's flagship and ships under a clean MIT license, while Qwen wins on breadth, documented multilingual reach and laptop-friendly open weights. Pick DeepSeek for cheap, heavy reasoning and coding at scale; pick Qwen when you need many model sizes, 200-plus languages, or a model you can run on one machine.

DeepSeek or Qwen — which should I pick?
DeepSeek for the cheapest serious reasoning and coding at API scale; Qwen for model-size choice, 201-language coverage and small open weights you can host locally.
Which is cheaper on the API?
DeepSeek, clearly. V4-Flash lists about $0.14 in / $0.28 out per 1M tokens, while Qwen's flagship Qwen3.8-Max runs $2 / $6. Qwen's own Flash tier is close to DeepSeek on price but weaker on capability.
Which is better at coding?
DeepSeek edges the head-to-head on vendor-reported Terminal-Bench scores (V4-Pro near 87.9 vs Qwen3.8-Max near 86.6). Both are top open models; the gap is small and task-specific.
Which is more open?
Both publish weights, but licenses differ. DeepSeek's recent models are uniformly MIT. Qwen mixes Apache 2.0 (small models) with a custom licence on its largest flagships that adds terms above certain revenue or user thresholds.
Which is better for non-English and Chinese?
Qwen, on the numbers. Alibaba documents 201 languages and dialects; DeepSeek has never published a comparable figure. For Chinese, both are strong, but Qwen's multilingual breadth is the one with evidence behind it.
Can I run either locally?
Only Qwen at the small end. Qwen3.8-27B (Apache 2.0) and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B fit a single GPU; DeepSeek's current open weights are server-class, hundreds of gigabytes, needing multi-GPU rigs.
Do they both do reasoning?
Yes. DeepSeek turns thinking on by default with a reasoning_effort knob; Qwen uses an enable_thinking switch on its hybrid models, which also carries prior reasoning into later turns.
Does PickWorth take money from either lab?
No. This comparison is written by one editorial team; pricing and model facts were checked against each vendor's official pages and independent model cards.

Reasoning and thinking

Both families treat reasoning as a mode, not a separate product, and that is the right call for a comparison: you are not choosing between a thinker and a chatterer, you are choosing how each lab exposes the thinking. DeepSeek switches thinking on by default and lets you dial it with a reasoning_effort setting of low, high or max; the API returns the chain alongside the answer. Qwen puts an enable_thinking flag on its hybrid models and adds preserve_thinking, which carries earlier reasoning into later turns and bills it as input. In practice DeepSeek's default-on stance means you get reasoning without thinking about it, while Qwen gives you finer control and a cleaner story for long agent sessions where prior steps matter.

The one caveat worth repeating: every score here is vendor-reported. Neither lab submits to a single independent harness with identical settings, so a 1.3-point Terminal-Bench gap between V4-Pro and Qwen3.8-Max is a rounding error next to the differences in your own prompts. Run the two on ten real tasks before you trust a benchmark.

Coding

On paper DeepSeek is ahead. Its own model cards put V4-Pro at about 87.9 on Terminal-Bench 2.1 and the smaller V4.1-Flash even higher at 90.6 under maximum reasoning effort, against Qwen3.8-Max at roughly 86.6. DeepSeek also has the longer open-reasoning pedigree, going back to the R1 release that made cheap inference famous. Qwen's answer is Qwen3-Coder and its agent tooling, plus first-party Qwen Code, which some teams prefer for the tighter Alibaba-hosted loop.

For fully local coding the picture flips. Qwen publishes a 27B dense model that fits an 18GB Ollama build on a single 24GB card; DeepSeek's current open weights run 160 to almost 900 gigabytes and are server-class. If the code has to stay on your machine, Qwen is the only one of the two with a current consumer-size build, and you give up little on everyday tasks.

Multilingual and English

This is the clearest gap and the one Qwen wins outright. Alibaba documents 201 languages and dialects for the Qwen3.5 class, and that breadth carries into the newer family; DeepSeek has never published a comparable count. For English and Chinese both are fluent, and DeepSeek's English is strong enough that plenty of Western teams use it as their default open model. But if your product serves Arabic, Indonesian, Portuguese or any of the long tail, Qwen is the safer bet because the coverage is stated, not assumed. DeepSeek's silence on language count is not proof it is weak — it is just unstated, and in a comparison the stated number wins.

The cost question

DeepSeek's pricing is the headline. Straight from its platform page, V4-Flash is $0.0028 per 1M tokens on a cache hit, $0.14 on a miss and $0.28 out; V4-Pro is $0.003625 / $0.435 / $0.87. Qwen's flagship Qwen3.8-Max lists around $2 in / $6 out per 1M on Alibaba's Model Studio, with Qwen3.7-Plus at $0.40 / $1.60 and Qwen3.8-Flash at $0.15 / $0.47. So DeepSeek's cheap tier undercuts Qwen's cheap tier slightly and its flagship undercuts Qwen's flagship by roughly 4x on output. For high-volume RAG, agents and coding assistants the arithmetic is brutal: a million-call chatbot that costs a few hundred dollars on DeepSeek-Flash can cost over a thousand on Qwen's mid tier. The counter is that Qwen's Flash and DeepSeek's Flash land near the same price, so the cheap end of each is comparable and the real split shows up at the top.

Open weights and breadth

Both publish weights, which is the whole point of the category, but the licences and the lineup tell the story. DeepSeek keeps it simple: every recent model, from V3.2 through the V4 family and the older R1, ships under MIT. You can fine-tune, host and resell without reading a clause twice. Qwen splits by size. The small checkpoints, Qwen3.8-27B and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, are Apache 2.0 and genuinely friendly. The flagships carry a custom licence with extra terms once you pass about 100 million monthly users or $20 million in monthly revenue, and Qwen3.7-Plus is API-only with no open weights at all. Breadth is Qwen's real advantage: one vendor covers text, vision, audio and video, while DeepSeek stays text-first. If you want a model zoo from a single source, Qwen is the wider door; if you want one licence and one engine done well, DeepSeek is.

Pick by scenario

Choose DeepSeek when the work is a firehose of reasoning or code through an API and the bill matters. Chatbots, RAG, coding agents and any batch job that runs millions of calls will be dramatically cheaper, and the MIT licence removes the revenue-trigger worry. Choose Qwen when you need more than one shape of model: a small one for on-premise privacy, a flagship for hard tasks, and vision or audio without bolt-ons. Choose Qwen again if your users are outside English and Chinese, because the 201-language claim is the only one with a number behind it. And choose Qwen if you must self-host on modest hardware, since DeepSeek's current open weights simply will not fit. Most comparison readers are buying an API, which is why DeepSeek takes the higher score here, but the honest answer is that the two are complements more than rivals and a shop running both is not unusual.
