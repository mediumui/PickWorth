Three jobs, three tools

"Research" in an AI tool usually means one of three things, and they are not interchangeable. Finding out — what is true, what happened, what the current number is — is a search problem, and the right tool searches by default and cites what it found. Reading — a stack of PDFs, a long report, a transcript — is a context problem, and the right tool holds a large document set without losing the thread. Writing — turning what you read into a summary or an argument — is a generation problem, and that is the general assistant's home ground. The mistake is buying one tool for all three jobs and being mediocre at each.

Perplexity: the cited answer engine

Perplexity is the tool whose free tier is a complete product, which in this category is the single most important fact. Every answer arrives with its sources attached, which means you can verify a claim instead of trusting the model's confidence — and for research, verifiability is the whole point. It searches by default, it collapses the twelve-tabs-and-a-scratchpad loop into one window, and the paid tier mostly buys you more of the same at higher limits and on the stronger models. If your week is heavy on finding out, this is the subscription that earns its place first.

The general assistants, when search is enough

ChatGPT and Gemini both search, and both are adequate when the question is simple and you are already inside the assistant doing something else. Neither is source-first: the search happens when the model decides it is needed, and the answer does not arrive with a citation list you can audit. That is fine for the second half of research — drafting the summary, restructuring the notes, writing the email — and it is the wrong shape for the first half. Use them for the writing job, not the finding-out job.

Long-context readers: a lot at once

When the input is not a question but a pile — forty pages of filings, a set of transcripts, a long technical document plus its comments — the job is context, not search. This is where Claude's long-context handling earns its tier: it holds structure across a very large input and degrades more gracefully than the rest when the document set gets big. If your research is reading-heavy rather than question-heavy, this is the tier that pays for itself, and it is the one most researchers underuse because they default to the search tool out of habit.

The setup that actually works

The practical answer is a chain, not a single winner. Perplexity (free is fine) to find out what is true and to get the sources in one click. A long-context reader when the input is a document pile rather than a question. A general assistant to turn the result into something you can hand to someone else. Run in that order, each tool does the one job it is best at, and the total cost is one free tier plus whichever paid tier matches your volume.
