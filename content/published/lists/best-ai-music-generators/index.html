Buyer's guide

The best AI music generators in 2026

AI music split into two camps this year: the song factories that hand you a finished, sung track, and the free or open experiments that cost nothing but give you less. Suno and Udio lead the first camp and trade wins on speed versus fidelity. Google's Lyria, Stable Audio and Riffusion cover the rest. Here is where each one actually earns a place in your workflow.
Updated 26 October 2026 PickWorth Editorial
3.9Our score

The short version

Start with Suno unless you already know why you would not. It is the only front-runner that gives a beginner a complete, downloadable, commercially licensable song in under a minute, and its free tier is generous enough to decide without paying. Move to Udio when the singing has to fool a musician, accept that you are renting the listening experience rather than owning the file. Reach for Google's Lyria or MusicFX when the budget is zero and the watermark is acceptable. Choose Stable Audio when the deliverable is a sound effect bed or an instrumental you plan to clear for client work, and keep Riffusion in your back pocket as the free, open sandbox. The category is good, not great: the song factories sound convincing but the licensing and export stories are still settling, so treat any paid plan as a subscription you can leave rather than a studio you have built.

The short answer. For most people Suno is the right first stop because it ships complete, downloadable songs fastest, but pick Udio when vocal realism matters, Google's Lyria or MusicFX when your budget is zero, Stable Audio for sound effects and commercial instrumentals, and Riffusion when you want to tinker for free.

Two camps, not one list

The comparison most buyers attempt, Suno versus Udio, misses the shape of the field. Five tools here do five different jobs, and ranking them on a single axis produces a chart that is wrong for almost everyone. On one side sit the song factories: type a prompt, get a two-to-four-minute track with vocals, structure and a mix. Suno and Udio live here. On the other side sit the free and open options that trade polish for zero cost or total control: Google's Lyria, Stable Audio and Riffusion. Know which camp your task belongs to before reading a single score.

One caveat runs through the whole category. "Free" almost always means "not yours to sell." Every free tier below limits or forbids commercial use, and several vendors are still fighting training-data lawsuits that could reshape who owns what. Budget for at least one paid plan the moment money touches the music.

Suno: the default song machine

Best for: complete songs with the least friction

Suno is the tool most newcomers should open first. Describe a track in a sentence, hit generate, and within a minute you get a finished song with lyrics, a sung vocal, instrumentation and a coherent verse-chorus structure. The interface is forgiving, the community library is enormous, and the iteration loop is fast: regenerate a section, extend the track, remix a style without leaving the browser.

The official pricing page lists three plans. Free gives 50 credits a day, roughly ten songs, on the standard model with no commercial rights and a shared queue. Pro at $8 a month raises that to 2,500 credits (about 500 songs), adds commercial-use rights for songs made while subscribed, stem separation and priority generation. Premier at $24 a month brings 10,000 credits and Suno Studio, a browser-based workstation with MIDI, effects and automation for producers who want to keep working the file.

Where Suno shows its seams: high vocal notes can pick up a metallic hum, complex arrangements occasionally muddy, and the free tier's creations stay non-commercial forever even if you upgrade later. For mainstream pop, hip-hop and country, though, it is the fastest path from idea to owned file.

Udio: the fidelity pick

Best for: vocals that fool a musician

Udio consistently produces the most natural, expressive AI singing in blind listening, with cleaner mixes and better phrasing than its rival. Custom lyrics land the way you wrote them instead of being paraphrased, and the extend-and-remix workflow lets you build a track section by section. On pure audio quality, especially for electronic, ambient and instrumental genres, it edges Suno.

Pricing tracks Suno closely. Free allows 10 credits a day plus 100 a month (about four short songs, capped at three full-length generations daily). Standard at $10 a month unlocks 2,400 credits, longer songs without the daily cap, voice control and lyric editing. Pro at $30 a month raises the pool to 6,000 credits and the highest generation limits.

The catch is large and easy to miss. Following Udio's October 2025 settlement with Universal Music Group, downloads, stem exports and video exports are disabled on every tier, free and paid alike; the platform is streaming-only while a licensed rebuild is in progress. You can listen, share and remix inside Udio, but you cannot take the file with you. For a hobbyist that is fine; for anyone who needs a master to ship, it is a dealbreaker today. We score Udio on what it sounds like, not on what it lets you keep.

Google Lyria 3.5 and MusicFX: the free play

Best for: zero-cost short-form and ambient music

If the price is the whole decision, Google wins. Lyria 3.5 sits inside the Gemini app and is free for any user over 18, generating 30-second tracks on Lyria 3 and two-to-three-minute songs on the 3.5 update, with auto-written lyrics, cover art and support for text, image or video prompts. Higher limits go to paid Gemini subscribers, but the base access costs nothing.

MusicFX, the separate Google Labs experiment built on the same Lyria model, is the better tool for live, instrumental work: it generates music in real time with no caps and no paywall, letting you push faders and layer prompts while the sound plays. It suits focus music and live-stream beds more than finished songs.

Two limits keep Google off the podium for serious work. Every track carries an imperceptible SynthID watermark that flags it as AI-made and survives compression and edits, so the origin is never a secret. And Google has published no commercial licence or service-level guarantee; there is no way to buy reliability or clearance. Great for Shorts, Reels and TikTok, thin for client deliverables.

Stable Audio: the producer's bench

Best for: sound effects and cleared instrumentals

Stable Audio, from the Stable Diffusion team at Stability AI, is the quiet workhorse of the category. Where rivals chase sung pop, Stable Audio shines at instrumental tracks, ambient beds and a dedicated sound-effects engine, footsteps, explosions, UI blips, that few competitors bother with. Paid plans ship full commercial rights, and a developer API bills per credit for product teams embedding generation.

The free tier allows ten track generations a month on a personal, non-commercial licence, restricted in some regions. Pro at $11.99 a month lifts that to 500 generations with 30 minutes of monthly audio upload and commercial rights; Studio at $29.99 a month reaches 1,500 generations. Unused generations do not roll over.

The trade is vocal quality. Stable Audio's sung output trails Suno and Udio, so bring it when the brief is a bed or an effect, not a singer. For video editors, game studios and filmmakers who need a royalty-clear asset they can actually license, it is the safest paid pick after the song factories.

Riffusion: free and open

Best for: learning and tinkering at zero cost

Riffusion started as a clever hack, fine-tuning Stable Diffusion to paint audio spectrograms, and it remains the most transparent tool on the list. The core model is open-source with weights on GitHub, free to run with no account and no hard usage cap. Version 2, shipped in early 2026, raised quality to 44.1kHz stereo and extended clips toward 30 seconds.

That freedom has a ceiling. Riffusion is instrumental only, no vocals, and outputs short clips that must be stitched into longer pieces. Quality sits below the paid song factories, and self-hosting demands a GPU with at least 8GB of VRAM. As a research toy, a classroom resource or a sandbox for budding builders, nothing beats free with the source in hand. As a delivery tool, it is not the call.

Pick by job

The verdicts that actually matter

Best overall: Suno. It is the only front-runner that pairs speed, a usable free tier and exportable, licensable files, which is what most first-time buyers want.

Best free: Google Lyria 3.5 in Gemini, with MusicFX for ambient and live work. No card, no cap, good enough for social clips.

Best vocals: Udio, by a hair, for natural tone and clean mixing, provided you can live inside the app and never need the file.

Best for experimentation: Riffusion, free and open, ideal for learning the mechanics or self-hosting your own instance.

Best for sound effects and cleared instrumentals: Stable Audio, the producer's bench when the deliverable is a bed, not a singer.

Pick the job before the tool. The leaderboard changes monthly as these models ship, but the split between song factory and free experiment is the only frame that keeps the choice honest.
