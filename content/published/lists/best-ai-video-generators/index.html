Six tools, one question: what is the job?

The video category is not a single leaderboard. Each tool below is built for a different step of the work: some generate a good clip from a prompt, some animate a still image, some hand you an editor so the clip becomes a finished video, and some win on price so you can render a hundred variants. Asking which is "best" without naming the job produces a ranking that fits nobody. The short version: Google Veo 3.1 makes the best-looking clip with sound, Runway is the only one here with a real editing timeline, Kling gives the most generous free tier and the lowest cost per second, Luma is the image-to-video and long-clip specialist, and Pika is the cheap, playful effects toy. Sora, the name everyone searched a year ago, no longer exists.

Runway: best when the video has to be edited

Runway is the only tool in this list that behaves like a post-production suite rather than a clip vending machine. Its Gen-4 and Gen-4.5 models feed a browser timeline where you cut, mask, track motion, paint out objects, and drive camera moves with the motion brush. If your deliverable is a finished 30-second ad rather than one pretty shot, that editor is worth more than a marginally better raw model. Character consistency from a reference image is genuinely strong, which is why agencies keep coming back.

The cost is twofold. Paid plans start at $12 a month for 625 credits and remove the watermark at 1080p; Pro at $28 adds 4K upscale and 2,250 credits; Unlimited at $76 drops the counter but throttles speed at peak. Credits burn fast: a 10-second 1080p Gen-4.5 clip can cost around 100 credits, so Standard covers roughly six to twelve clips a month. And every generation caps near 10 seconds, so longer pieces are stitch jobs. The free tier is 125 credits total and never refills, so treat it as a trial, not a plan.

Kling: best free tier and best value

Kling, from Kuaishou, is the value pick and the free-tier winner. The free plan drops 66 credits into your account every day, enough for several five-second clips, though free output carries a watermark, blocks commercial use, and sits at the back of the queue. Paid Standard runs about $10 a month for 660 credits and Pro about $37 for 3,000. Where Kling earns its rank is physics: hair, fabric, liquid, and camera motion hold up better than most rivals, and on-screen text (logos, signs, labels) stays legible instead of melting, a real advantage for product and e-commerce shots. Kling 3.0 also adds a multi-shot storyboard mode with native audio synced across cuts.

Two caveats. High-quality generations queue slowly even on paid tiers during peak hours, and the longest single clip is about 15 seconds. Sign-up friction varies by region, with the mainland site reported to ask for a Chinese phone number in some cases, though international access has widened. For most creators balancing quality against budget, Kling is the sweet spot, and its daily free credits make it the safest first stop.

Luma Dream Machine: best for image-to-video and long clips

Luma's Ray 3.14 model is the specialist for turning a still into motion. Its lighting physics, HDR output, and smooth camera moves are the most convincing in this group for atmospheric, cinematic shots, and it supports start-and-end keyframes so you can control exactly how a scene resolves. Clip length is the differentiator: Luma stretches to roughly 60 seconds, far beyond Runway's 10 or Kling's 15, which matters if you are animating a product hero shot or a slow landscape.

The free plan gives around 30 generations a month, watermarked and personal-use only, so it is a sandbox rather than a production line; the Lite plan at $9.99 unlocks watermark-free output, and Plus at $29.99 adds commercial rights and HDR. The gap is audio: Luma generates video only, no native sound, so any voice or score is a separate step. Peak-hour queues on free and Lite tiers can stretch to many minutes. If your work starts from an image and you want it to breathe, Luma is the call; if you need dialogue, look at Veo.

Google Veo 3.1 and Gemini Flow: best raw quality, with audio

Veo 3.1 is the quality leader and the only model here that generates lip-synced audio in the same pass as the video. For talking-head explainers, product demos, or any clip where flat "AI demo" sound breaks the illusion, that native audio is the feature that justifies the pick. Google ships it through Flow, a filmmaking studio with scene building and object insert or remove tools. The free tier is 50 Flow credits a day for any Google account, which covers a few Veo 3.1 Lite or Fast clips; Quality costs 100 credits and is out of reach on free. Paid access runs through Google AI plans: Plus at $4.99 adds 200 monthly credits, Pro at $19.99 adds 1,000, and the Ultra tiers scale to 25,000.

The catches are real. In the Gemini app, video generation now uses Gemini Omni rather than Veo and requires a paid plan, so free Veo inside a chatbot is not a thing. Flow's editing tools are blocked in the EEA, Switzerland, and the UK. Daily credits never roll over, and peak hours (roughly 2 to 5 PM UTC) can refuse generation without a subscription. At the API, Veo 3.1 Standard runs about $0.40 per second, Fast about $0.10 to 0.12, Lite about $0.05 to 0.08. For enterprise compliance work, Vertex AI is the cleanest landing spot.

Pika: best for playful effects and the cheapest paid entry

Pika is the lightweight, consumer-facing option, and it owns the "make something fun fast" corner. Its strengths are video-to-video effects: Pikaffects can melt, inflate, or explode objects, Pikaswaps swap elements, and Pikaframes chains longer sequences. The interface is the friendliest here, and on cost per second it is the cheapest in the category: Standard at $10 a month works out to roughly $0.057 per second at 720p, about a fifth of Runway's rate on a lower-tier model.

The free plan is packs-only with zero monthly credits, though downloads are watermark-free; commercial rights require a paid plan (Standard $10, Creator $35, Fancy $95). Free and Standard cap at 480p, so 720p or 1080p pushes you up a tier. Rendering realism trails Veo and Runway, and clips stay short. Use Pika when the brief is a stylized social clip or a quick effect, not a photorealistic scene.

Sora is gone: what that means for you

OpenAI retired Sora. The app and web experience shut down on 26 April 2026, and the API followed on 24 September 2026; OpenAI's own help center confirms both dates and tells users to export before access ends. If you built a workflow on Sora, migrate now. The closest replacements by job: Veo 3.1 for raw quality and audio, Kling 3.0 for value and iteration, Runway for editing and control. ChatGPT no longer offers Sora as a video feature, and the Gemini app's video now defaults to Gemini Omni rather than Veo, so do not assume your chatbot still makes the clip you remember. The lesson is vendor commitment: a model's quality is a snapshot, a platform's survival is the investment.

The picks, by scenario

Best quality with sound: Google Veo 3.1. Best free tier: Kling, with 66 daily credits. Best value per second: Kling again, or Pika at the very bottom of the price ladder. Best for editing a finished video: Runway. Best for animating a still image and for the longest single clip: Luma Dream Machine. Best for playful social effects on the cheap: Pika. Replacing Sora: Veo for quality, Kling for value, Runway for control. None of these is "the best" in a vacuum; pick the one whose limit you will never hit.
