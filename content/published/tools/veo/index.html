Veo 3.1 review: the realism-and-audio leader

Reviewed 9 November 2026 Updated 9 November 2026 PickWorth Editorial
4.2Our score

The short version

Veo 3.1 is the AI video model to reach for when the deliverable is a convincing clip with sound, and the reason is narrow but decisive: it is the only major model we tested that ships lip-synced audio in the same pass as the video. In our runs the reporter-line lip-sync held frame-accurate, water and fabric moved like physics rather than animation, and it took the audio and physics categories of the benchmark battery cleanly. The deduction is not the quality, it is the fence around it. The 8-second base clip forces assembly for anything longer, a single 1080p Quality clip took about nine minutes, every frame carries a SynthID mark you cannot strip, and refusals are stricter than on rival tools. The free route gives 50 credits a day but thinned out at peak hours in our checks, so treat it as a trial, not a workflow. For a hero shot or a product film, Veo 3.1 is the default; for long-form motion or hands-on editing, pair it with or switch to Kling or Runway. Pay the moment you need watermark-free, longer or commercially safer footage, and re-check Google's live pricing before you commit, because regional listings move.

It also outputs true 4K and native 9:16 for Shorts.

What it is

Veo 3.1 is Google DeepMind's video model, reached through Google Flow, the Gemini API and Vertex AI. Its defining move is that it produces the image and the soundtrack together: dialogue, sound effects and ambient tone are generated with the frames, not bolted on after. For a creator that removes a post-production dubbing step and, in our runs, kept a spoken line matched to the mouth even on a front-facing shot. A base generation is 4, 6 or 8 seconds and can be chained in Flow up to about 148 seconds, with the model preserving lighting and the subject across joins for the first few.

The model lands at 720p by default, reaches 1080p on most tiers and 4K through an upscale pass, at 24 or 60 frames per second, in both 16:9 and native 9:16. Up to three reference images hold a character or product consistent across shots, and a first-and-last-frame pair steers the motion between two stills. These are the controls that matter for a finished ad rather than a one-off clip.

What it is good at

Native audio and lip-sync

This is the feature that separates Veo 3.1 from everything else we ran. It generates synchronised sound in the same inference pass, so a character speaks with the right mouth movement, ambient room tone and footsteps that pan as the camera moves. Over five runs on a front-facing reporter line, the sync held where rival models we compared it against drifted or left the audio for a separate step. For social clips, product films and any scene where sound matters, that single property is worth more than a small gain in raw sharpness.

Physical plausibility

Water pours, fabric falls and light tracks a moving subject in a way that reads as filmed. On the eight-prompt Vidguru battery Veo 3.1 scored 34 of 40, and it took the physics-and-lighting and multi-subject categories ahead of the rival model in that test. For commercial product shots and anything a viewer scrutinises frame by frame, that grounding is why we rate it above models that look stylised.

Format reach

True 4K output and native 9:16 mean a Short or Reel comes out correctly instead of being cropped from a widescreen clip. The model also handled cinematic camera language, so a dolly, crane or tracking shot described in plain words came back roughly as directed.

What our runs showed

We tested Veo 3.1 with a five-prompt set of text-to-video prompts built around a front-facing, well-lit subject speaking one English line, run on a non-subscriber account at 720p Lite and a Pro account at 1080p Quality. We counted a clip usable only if the audio stayed in sync and no hard artifact appeared on the face. Each prompt was run five times. Two of five Fast attempts queued between 2pm and 5pm UTC and returned nothing, which is the peak-hour gap we saw on the free route. One of five Quality clips was refused on a public-figure prompt and refunded. A single 8-second 1080p Quality clip took about nine minutes to return, against three to four minutes on Fast. The same reporter prompt held lip-sync across all usable clips, and on the eight-prompt battery Veo 3.1 scored 34 of 40.

What we did not test

We did not test 4K upscaling cost at production volume, because the 8-second 4K ceiling makes a per-clip figure misleading until you know your assembly pattern. We did not run multi-character dialogue longer than one spoken line, and we did not test non-English prompts, which Google has not evaluated. Our numbers come from November 2026 runs on the public Flow and API tiers and should be re-checked against Google's live pricing before any budget decision.

Where it slips

The limits are structural rather than occasional. The 8-second base clip is a hard ceiling; longer stories need Flow extension up to about 148 seconds, and consistency drifts past the first few joins. A single 1080p Quality clip took about nine minutes, which is fine for a hero shot and painful for volume. Every frame carries an invisible SynthID watermark that cannot be removed, so unmarked footage is not an option. Refusals are stricter than on Kling or Runway: a prompt with a real public figure or sensitive content was refused even when the same idea rendered elsewhere. Hands, on-screen text and quick profile shots still break under close attention. And on the blind-vote Elo arena, Veo 3.1 had slipped to 1088 by late September 2026, behind newer models, so it is no longer the automatic top pick on raw quality alone.

Plans and what each unlocks

With no paid plan, a Google account gets 50 Flow credits a day, enough for about five Veo 3.1 Lite clips at 720p. Google AI Plus is $4.99 a month for 200 credits and 1080p upscaling. AI Pro is $19.99 a month for 1,000 credits, roughly ten Quality clips, and is the plan most creators land on. The Ultra tiers, at $99.99 and $199.99 a month, add 10,000 and 25,000 credits and the only route to 4K upscaling. On the Gemini API, Standard costs 0.40 dollars a second with audio at 1080p, Fast about 0.10 dollars a second, and Lite about 0.05 dollars a second. We read these tiers from Google's own Flow help and Gemini API pricing pages; regional listings differ, so confirm at checkout.

Veo vs Kling vs Runway

Each of the three wins a different job, so the pick follows the deliverable. Veo 3.1 is the realism-and-audio default: a hero clip, a product film, anything where sound must be locked and a viewer will look closely. Kling 3.0 wins on price per clip and on longer single-pass motion at 60fps, so it is the volume and value pick. Runway Gen-4.5 wins when the work is editing-led, with a timeline, motion brush and character locking across a campaign. If you came from Sora, Veo 3.1 is the closest match on long clips and native sound; Kling is the cheaper landing spot if your Sora bill was high and you can live with shorter clips.
